Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning · Chapter 18 · §18.5.5 – §18.6

Each new tree is one gradient step. Then we ask the ensemble what it learned.

The last lab built a separate boosting algorithm for each loss: residuals for squared error, AdaBoost for exponential loss, LogitBoost for log loss. Gradient boosting (§18.5.5) replaces all of them with one recipe. Compute how each training prediction should move to lower the loss (the negative gradient), fit a small regression tree to those numbers, and add a little of that tree to the model.

Gradient tree boosting (18.5.5.1) then re-tunes each leaf for the real loss, and XGBoost (18.5.5.2) uses curvature as well as slope, plus a price on every leaf. A boosted ensemble of hundreds of trees can't be read like one tree, so §18.6 gives two ways to look inside: feature importance and partial dependence plots.

§18.5.5

Gradient boosting: gradient descent where each step is a tree

We want \(\hat f=\arg\min_f\mathcal L(f)\). Treat the function itself as the thing to optimize, and represent it by its values on the training set, \(\mathbf f=(f(\mathbf x_1),\dots,f(\mathbf x_N))\). Then gradient descent is ordinary gradient descent on an \(N\)-vector. The gradient at step \(m\) is

\[ g_{im}=\left[\frac{\partial\ell(y_i,f(\mathbf x_i))}{\partial f(\mathbf x_i)}\right]_{f=f_{m-1}},\qquad \mathbf f_m=\mathbf f_{m-1}-\beta_m\mathbf g_m,\qquad \beta_m=\arg\min_\beta\mathcal L(\mathbf f_{m-1}-\beta\mathbf g_m). \]

That only moves \(f\) at the \(N\) training points, so nothing generalizes. The fix is to fit a weak learner to \(-\mathbf g_m\) and step along the learner instead. That is Algorithm 18.3. With squared loss, \(-g_{im}=y_i-f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)\) is the residual, so you get back L2Boosting. Swap in absolute or Huber loss and the same code does robust regression.

Explain itAt your level

It's a game of "warmer, colder," played by a team of helpers.

You are trying to draw a line that goes through a bunch of dots. A friend looks at each dot and says "your line should go up here" or "down here," and how strongly. Those shouts are the gradient.

You could just move your line to touch every dot exactly. But then if someone asks about a new spot between the dots, you have no idea what to say, because you only fixed the dots you saw.

So you hire a helper who can only learn simple rules, like "everything on the left should go up a bit, everything on the right should go down." The helper listens to the shouts and makes the best simple rule it can. You move your line a little in that direction. Then the friend shouts again, and a new helper makes a new rule. After a hundred helpers, your line fits well, even in between the dots.

The friend can shout in different ways. With squared error, a dot that is far away shouts very, very loudly. With absolute error, every dot just says "up" or "down" at the same volume, so one weird dot can't take over the game.

Gradient descent: to go downhill, step opposite to the slope. Here, the thing we move is the list of predictions \(f(x_1),\dots,f(x_N)\), one number per training point. The "slope" for point \(i\) tells us which way to nudge \(f(x_i)\) to make the loss smaller.

For squared error \(\tfrac12(y-f)^2\), the slope is \(-(y-f)\), so going downhill means adding the residual \(y-f\). Do that once and the predictions equal the data exactly. That sounds great, but it's just memorizing. At a new \(x\) there is no prediction at all. Try "Raw gradient step" in the Play box and watch the test loss not move.

The fix: fit a small regression tree to the "which way to move" numbers. A tree gives an answer for every \(x\), not just the training points. Add a fraction \(\nu\) (like 0.1) of that tree to the model and repeat.

Changing the loss only changes the numbers the tree is fit to. Absolute error gives just \(+1\) or \(-1\) for each point. Huber gives the residual, but capped. Both stop outliers from dragging the model around.

Algorithm 18.3, line by line:

  1. \(f_0\) is the best constant: the mean for squared loss, the median for absolute loss, the log odds of the base rate for log loss.
  2. Pseudo-residuals \(r_{im}=-\partial\ell(y_i,f)/\partial f\big|_{f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)}\). These are the \(-\mathbf g_m\) of Eq. 18.38.
  3. Fit \(F_m=\arg\min_F\sum_i(r_{im}-F(\mathbf x_i))^2\). This is the least-squares projection of the gradient direction onto what a tree can represent, so it generalizes to new \(\mathbf x\).
  4. Update \(f_m=f_{m-1}+\nu F_m\). The book drops the line search for \(\beta_m\) (Eq. 18.40) and uses a fixed shrinkage \(\nu\in(0,1]\) instead, which regularizes. Smaller \(\nu\) needs more rounds but usually tests better.

The weak learner is always a regression model, even for classification, because pseudo-residuals are real numbers. That is why one implementation covers every differentiable loss in Table 18.1.

  • Steepest descent in a function class. With the empirical inner product \(\langle u,v\rangle=\frac1N\sum_iu(\mathbf x_i)v(\mathbf x_i)\), the least-squares fit picks the tree (up to scale) most aligned with \(-\mathbf g\). Mason et al.'s AnyBoost and Friedman's gradient boosting are the same idea.
  • Shrinkage as regularization. With small \(\nu\), forward stagewise fitting traces something close to the lasso path over the dictionary of trees (Efron et al. 2004; Rosset, Zhu & Hastie). The number of rounds \(M\) is the regularization knob, usually set by early stopping on validation loss.
  • Table 18.1 conventions. For exponential loss, \(-\partial\ell/\partial f=+\tilde y\,e^{-\tilde yf}\). The printed table has the sign of the derivative instead. For binary log loss with \(\pi=\sigma(2f)\) and \(\ell=\log(1+e^{-2\tilde yf})\), the negative gradient is \(2(y-\pi)\). With \(f\) as the full log odds and \(\pi=\sigma(f)\), it is \(y-\pi\), which is what libraries use and what this page uses.
  • In practice. scikit-learn's GradientBoostingRegressor(loss=...) offers squared_error, absolute_error, huber and quantile. The Huber \(\delta\) there is the alpha-quantile of \(|r|\), recomputed every round. This page fixes \(\delta=0.5\).

Math, slowlyEquations 18.38–18.41 and Algorithm 18.3

From functional gradient to a tree

  1. Write the empirical loss as a function of an \(N\)-vector: \(\mathcal L(\mathbf f)=\sum_{i=1}^N\ell(y_i,f_i)\). Each term depends on one coordinate only, so the gradient is coordinate-wise: \(g_{im}=\partial\ell(y_i,f_i)/\partial f_i\) at \(f_i=f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)\). (Eq. 18.38)
  2. Steepest descent: \(\mathbf f_m=\mathbf f_{m-1}-\beta_m\mathbf g_m\), with \(\beta_m\) from a 1-D line search. (Eqs. 18.39–18.40) For squared loss \(\beta=1\) lands on \(\mathbf f=\mathbf y\) in one step: perfect fit, zero generalization.
  3. Replace \(-\mathbf g_m\) by the closest function a tree can make: \(F_m=\arg\min_F\sum_i(-g_{im}-F(\mathbf x_i))^2\). (Eq. 18.41 / line 4) \(F_m\) is defined everywhere, so the step moves predictions at new inputs too.
  4. Take a small step: \(f_m=f_{m-1}+\nu F_m\). Line 5. The line search is optional [BH07].
  5. Squared loss: \(-g_{im}=y_i-f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)\), the residual, so Algorithm 18.3 is exactly least squares boosting (§18.5.2). Absolute loss: \(-g_{im}=\operatorname{sgn}(y_i-f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i))\), so each tree only learns which side each point is on.

§18.5.5

Classification, many classes, and stochastic gradient boosting

For binary classification use log loss. The pseudo-residual is \(y_i-\pi_i\), and the algorithm is called BinomialBoost [BH07]. Unlike LogitBoost it needs no weighted fit: any black-box regression model can fit the gradient vector. For \(C\) classes, keep \(C\) scores \(f_c\), fit \(C\) separate regression trees per round to

\[ -g_{icm}=\frac{\partial\ell(y_i,f_{1m}(\mathbf x_i),\dots,f_{Cm}(\mathbf x_i))}{\partial f_{cm}(\mathbf x_i)}=\mathbb I(y_i=c)-\pi_{ic},\qquad p(y=c\mid\mathbf x)=\frac{e^{f_c(\mathbf x)}}{\sum_{c'=1}^Ce^{f_{c'}(\mathbf x)}}. \]

On large datasets, give each tree a random fraction of the data, drawn without replacement. This is stochastic gradient boosting [Fri99]. It is faster and often generalizes better, since subsampling is a form of regularization.

Explain itAt your level

One team of helpers per color, and each team only cares about its own color.

Say there are red, blue and green dots. The red team keeps a "redness score" for every spot on the map, and the other teams do the same for their colors. Whichever score is biggest wins that spot.

After each round, every dot tells each team how surprised it was. A red dot that got a low redness score tells the red team "more red here!" and tells the others "less of you here!" Each team then adds a small helper to fix its own scores.

Sometimes we let each helper see only half of the dots, picked at random. That sounds worse, but it stops the helpers from memorizing every odd dot. It's like studying with different practice questions each night instead of the same ones over and over.

For two classes, the model's score \(f(x)\) turns into a probability with the sigmoid, \(\pi=\sigma(f)\). The pseudo-residual is just \(y-\pi\): the label (0 or 1) minus the predicted probability. If a spam email gets \(\pi=0.2\), its residual is \(+0.8\), "push my score up a lot." If it already gets \(0.95\), it says only \(+0.05\).

For \(C\) classes there are \(C\) scores and the softmax turns them into probabilities that add to 1. Each class gets its own tree per round, fit to "1 if this is my class, else 0, minus my probability."

Stochastic gradient boosting gives each round's trees a random fraction of the training points, for example half. Each tree is noisier, but the model fits the training noise less, and every round runs faster.

  • BinomialBoost vs LogitBoost. Both minimize log loss. LogitBoost (Alg. 18.2) takes a Newton step, so it needs a weighted least-squares fit to working responses \(z_i\) with weights \(\pi_i(1-\pi_i)\). BinomialBoost takes a plain gradient step and fits an unweighted tree to \(y_i-\pi_i\).
  • Multiclass (Eq. 18.42). Since \(\ell=-\sum_cy_{ic}\log\pi_{ic}\) and \(\partial\log\pi_{ic'}/\partial f_c=\mathbb I(c=c')-\pi_{ic}\), the gradient is \(\pi_{ic}-y_{ic}\). The \(C\) trees are fit separately but coupled through the softmax (Eq. 18.43).
  • The residuals sum to zero over classes: \(\sum_c(\mathbb I(y_i=c)-\pi_{ic})=1-1=0\). Adding the same constant to every \(f_c\) leaves the softmax unchanged, so one of the \(C\) scores is redundant.
  • Subsampling (fraction typically 0.5–0.8) works on top of shrinkage. Bagging lowers variance by averaging, and here the randomness in each step does something similar.
  • Leaf values. Friedman's multiclass TreeBoost doesn't keep the least-squares leaf means. It replaces them with a one-step Newton estimate \(\frac{C-1}{C}\frac{\sum r_{ic}}{\sum|r_{ic}|(1-|r_{ic}|)}\). The \((C-1)/C\) factor accounts for the redundant score. The demo keeps plain leaf means, as in Algorithm 18.3.
  • Identifiability. Fitting \(C\) trees wastes one tree's worth of capacity. Some implementations fit \(C-1\) and pin a reference class, but symmetric treatment is the default in scikit-learn, XGBoost (multi:softprob) and LightGBM.
  • Subsampling has extra uses. scikit-learn reports oob_improvement_ when subsample<1, an out-of-bag estimate of each round's gain. LightGBM's GOSS keeps all large-gradient points and samples the small-gradient ones, reweighting to keep the gradient unbiased.
  • Interaction with \(\nu\). Friedman (1999) found the biggest gains from subsampling with small shrinkage. With \(\nu=1\), the extra noise can hurt.

The softmax gradient

  1. Loss for one point with one-hot label \(y_{ic}\): \(\ell=-\sum_{c'}y_{ic'}\log\pi_{ic'}\), where \(\pi_{ic'}=e^{f_{c'}}/\sum_ke^{f_k}\).
  2. \(\log\pi_{ic'}=f_{c'}-\log\sum_ke^{f_k}\), so \(\dfrac{\partial\log\pi_{ic'}}{\partial f_c}=\mathbb I(c'=c)-\pi_{ic}\).
  3. \(\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial f_c}=-\sum_{c'}y_{ic'}\big(\mathbb I(c'=c)-\pi_{ic}\big)=-y_{ic}+\pi_{ic}\sum_{c'}y_{ic'}=\pi_{ic}-y_{ic}\).
  4. Negate: \(-g_{icm}=\mathbb I(y_i=c)-\pi_{ic}\). (Eq. 18.42) With \(C=2\) and \(f=f_1-f_0\), the softmax becomes the sigmoid and this is \(y_i-\pi_i\), the binary row of Table 18.1.
  5. Each round: \(f_{cm}=f_{c,m-1}+\nu F_{cm}\) for every \(c\), then \(p(y=c\mid\mathbf x)\propto e^{f_c(\mathbf x)}\). (Eq. 18.43)

§18.5.5.1

Gradient tree boosting: choose regions by gradient, set leaves by the loss

In practice the weak learner is almost always a regression tree, \(F_m(\mathbf x)=\sum_{j=1}^{J_m}w_{jm}\,\mathbb I(\mathbf x\in R_{jm})\) (Eq. 18.44). That combination is gradient boosted regression trees, also called gradient tree boosting or MART. It does two things per round:

  1. Find the regions \(R_{jm}\) with ordinary least-squares tree learning on the pseudo-residuals.
  2. Throw away the least-squares leaf values and re-solve each leaf for the actual loss: \(\hat w_{jm}=\arg\min_w\sum_{\mathbf x_i\in R_{jm}}\ell(y_i,f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+w)\) (Eq. 18.45).

For squared error, the optimal \(\hat w_{jm}\) is just the mean residual in the leaf, which is what the tree already had. For other losses it is not.

Explain itAt your level

First draw the neighborhoods, then pick the best number for each one.

The helper splits the map into a few neighborhoods by listening to the "up" and "down" shouts. That part is good at finding where things need fixing.

But with absolute error the shouts are only "up" or "down," never "up by a lot." So the helper's number for a neighborhood can't tell how far to move. We fix that by going back to each neighborhood and asking: "What single number, added to everyone here, would make the mistakes here smallest?" We use that number instead.

A regression tree has two parts: the splits (which neighborhood each point falls in) and the leaf values (one number per neighborhood).

Gradient tree boosting keeps the splits from the tree fit to the pseudo-residuals, but recomputes each leaf value for the real loss. For absolute error, the pseudo-residuals are all \(\pm1\), so the tree's leaf value is something like \(0.6\) ("most points here want to go up"). The best shift for absolute error is the median of the actual residuals in that leaf, which could be 0.05 or 2.3.

For squared error the tree's leaf value already is the mean residual, which is the best shift. So for squared error nothing changes.

Eq. 18.45 is a separate one-dimensional line search per leaf. The book's Eq. 18.40 searched one \(\beta\) for the whole function, so this gives every region its own step size. Closed forms:

  • Two different objectives. The regions are chosen by a first-order, least-squares criterion, but the leaf values come from the true loss (or a Newton approximation). XGBoost (next section) makes both use the same second-order objective.
  • Libraries. scikit-learn's GradientBoostingRegressor re-solves terminal regions for absolute, Huber and quantile losses (and uses Newton leaves for log loss). HistGradientBoosting, XGBoost and LightGBM use Newton leaves \(-G/(H+\lambda)\) throughout.
  • Vector leaves. In general \(w_{jm}\) can be a vector, for example one value per class in a single multi-output tree, instead of \(C\) separate trees.
  • MART (Friedman & Meulman 2003) is the same algorithm. The book expands the name as "multivariate additive regression trees" (Friedman's papers say "multiple"). Either way it stresses that the model is a sum of trees.

Math, slowlyEquations 18.44–18.45

Why the leaf problem splits apart

  1. With a tree, \(f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+F_m(\mathbf x_i)=f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+w_j\) for the one leaf \(j\) that \(\mathbf x_i\) falls in. (Eq. 18.44: exactly one indicator is 1)
  2. So \(\sum_i\ell(y_i,f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+F_m(\mathbf x_i))=\sum_j\sum_{\mathbf x_i\in R_{jm}}\ell(y_i,f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+w_j)\). Each \(w_j\) shows up in only one inner sum.
  3. Minimize each inner sum on its own: \(\hat w_{jm}=\arg\min_w\sum_{\mathbf x_i\in R_{jm}}\ell(y_i,f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+w)\). (Eq. 18.45)
  4. Squared loss: \(\frac{d}{dw}\sum\tfrac12(r_i-w)^2=0\Rightarrow\hat w=\bar r\), the mean residual in the leaf. Absolute loss: \(\sum|r_i-w|\) is minimized by the median of the \(r_i\).
  5. The model update is then \(f_m=f_{m-1}+\nu\sum_j\hat w_{jm}\mathbb I(\mathbf x\in R_{jm})\).

§18.5.5.2

XGBoost: a second-order step and a price on every leaf

XGBoost [CG16] adds a regularizer on tree complexity, uses a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss, samples features at internal nodes like a random forest, and uses systems tricks such as out-of-core computation to scale. Its objective is

\[ \mathcal L(f)=\sum_{i=1}^N\ell(y_i,f(\mathbf x_i))+\Omega(f),\qquad \Omega(f)=\gamma J+\tfrac12\lambda\sum_{j=1}^Jw_j^2 . \]

Expand the loss at step \(m\) to second order, with \(g_{im}\) and the Hessian \(h_{im}=\partial^2\ell/\partial f^2\) at \(f_{m-1}\), and group the points by leaf, with \(G_{jm}=\sum_{i\in I_j}g_{im}\) and \(H_{jm}=\sum_{i\in I_j}h_{im}\):

\[ w_j^*=-\frac{G_{jm}}{H_{jm}+\lambda},\qquad \mathcal L_m(q,\mathbf w^*)=-\frac12\sum_{j=1}^J\frac{G_{jm}^2}{H_{jm}+\lambda}+\gamma J, \]

\[ \text{gain}=\frac12\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda}+\frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda}-\frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda}\right]-\gamma . \]

A split that doesn't raise the bracket above \(\gamma\) has negative gain and isn't worth making.

Explain itAt your level

Every new neighborhood costs a coin, and big jumps make XGBoost nervous.

Before XGBoost splits a neighborhood in two, it asks: "How much better will things get?" If the answer is less than the price of one coin (called gamma), it doesn't split. That keeps trees small.

It's also careful about how big a number to give each neighborhood. If only a couple of dots live there, it trusts them less and keeps the number small. That's what lambda does.

And it doesn't only listen to "go up" or "go down." It also asks "how curvy is the hill here?" On a steep, curvy hill you take a small step. On a gentle, flat one you can take a bigger step.

Plain gradient boosting uses the slope \(g\) of the loss. XGBoost also uses the curvature \(h\), the second derivative. If you know the slope and the curvature, you can approximate the loss near your current guess by a parabola and jump to its lowest point. That is Newton's method, and it is where \(w^*=-G/(H+\lambda)\) comes from: total slope divided by total curvature in the leaf.

\(\lambda\) is added to the curvature, so it shrinks every leaf value toward 0. It shrinks small leaves (small \(H\)) the most. \(\gamma\) is a flat fee per leaf. The gain formula compares the score of the parent with the scores of the two children, and a split only happens if it beats the fee.

In the Play box, slide \(\lambda\) up and watch the leaf heights shrink. Slide \(\gamma\) up and watch splits disappear.

  • Squared loss check. \(\ell=\tfrac12(y-f)^2\) gives \(g_i=f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)-y_i=-r_i\) and \(h_i=1\). So \(w^*=\sum_{I_j}r_i/(n_j+\lambda)\), the mean residual shrunk toward 0. With \(\lambda=\gamma=0\), the gain is half the drop in squared error, and XGBoost grows the same tree as CART on the residuals.
  • Log loss. \(g_i=\pi_i-y_i\), \(h_i=\pi_i(1-\pi_i)\). Points the model is already sure about (\(\pi\) near 0 or 1) have tiny \(h\), so they count less in \(H\). Note that \(w^*\) with \(\lambda=0\) is exactly LogitBoost's Newton step.
  • Greedy growth. Eq. 18.56 is evaluated for every feature and every threshold, as in §18.1. The paper's approximate algorithm proposes candidate thresholds from weighted quantiles (weights \(h_i\)) instead of sorting every feature.
  • Other additions: column subsampling per tree or per node, row subsampling, a learned default direction for missing values, and cache-aware, out-of-core block structures.
  • Newton boosting. Regions and weights now share one objective. Empirically, Newton boosting converges in fewer rounds than gradient boosting for log loss. Sigrist (2021) compares them.
  • Bayesian reading of \(\lambda\). \(\tfrac12\lambda w^2\) is a Gaussian prior \(w\sim\mathcal N(0,1/\lambda)\) on leaf values, and \(-G/(H+\lambda)\) is the MAP Newton step.
  • Pruning. XGBoost grows each tree to max_depth and then prunes bottom-up any split whose gain is below 0. So a weak split can survive if a split below it is strong. The demo stops greedily instead. min_child_weight is a lower bound on \(H\) in each child, and max_delta_step caps \(|w|\), which helps for very imbalanced log loss where \(H\to0\).
  • Relatives. LightGBM uses histogram binning, leaf-wise (best-first) growth, GOSS and exclusive feature bundling. CatBoost uses ordered boosting to remove target leakage and symmetric (oblivious) trees.

Math, slowlyEquations 18.46–18.56

From Taylor expansion to the split gain

  1. At round \(m\), only \(F_m\) is free: \(\mathcal L_m(F_m)=\sum_i\ell(y_i,f_{m-1}(\mathbf x_i)+F_m(\mathbf x_i))+\Omega(F_m)+\text{const}\). (Eq. 18.48)
  2. Second-order Taylor in the small change \(F_m(\mathbf x_i)\): \(\ell(y_i,f_{m-1}+F)\approx\ell(y_i,f_{m-1})+g_{im}F+\tfrac12h_{im}F^2\). (Eqs. 18.49–18.50)
  3. Drop terms that don't depend on \(F_m\), and use \(F_m(\mathbf x)=w_{q(\mathbf x)}\): \(\sum_i\big[g_{im}w_{q(\mathbf x_i)}+\tfrac12h_{im}w_{q(\mathbf x_i)}^2\big]+\gamma J+\tfrac12\lambda\sum_jw_j^2\). (Eq. 18.51)
  4. Group by leaf: \(\sum_j\big[G_{jm}w_j+\tfrac12(H_{jm}+\lambda)w_j^2\big]+\gamma J\). (Eqs. 18.52–18.53) This is a separate quadratic in each \(w_j\).
  5. Set the derivative to zero: \(G_{jm}+(H_{jm}+\lambda)w_j=0\Rightarrow w_j^*=-G_{jm}/(H_{jm}+\lambda)\). (Eq. 18.54)
  6. Plug back in: \(G w^*+\tfrac12(H+\lambda)w^{*2}=-\frac{G^2}{H+\lambda}+\frac12\frac{G^2}{H+\lambda}=-\frac12\frac{G^2}{H+\lambda}\), giving Eq. 18.55, a score for any tree structure \(q\).
  7. Splitting leaf \(I\) into \(I_L\cup I_R\) replaces one term with two and adds one leaf (\(+\gamma\)). The loss drops by the gain in Eq. 18.56. Split only if the gain is positive.

§18.6 · 18.6.1

Interpreting tree ensembles: feature importance

One tree is easy to read. Hundreds of trees, whether bagged, a random forest or boosted, are not. A first question is which inputs the ensemble relies on. For one tree \(T\) [BFO84],

\[ R_k(T)=\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}G_j\,\mathbb I(v_j=k),\qquad R_k=\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^MR_k(T_m), \]

where the sum runs over internal nodes, \(G_j\) is the gain (drop in cost) at node \(j\), and \(v_j\) is the feature node \(j\) splits on. Averaging over the trees of the ensemble makes the estimate more reliable. Finally, rescale so the largest is 100%.

Explain itAt your level

Which clues did the trees use, and how much did each clue help?

Every time a tree asks a question, like "does the email have lots of exclamation marks?", the piles it makes get a little tidier: more spam on one side, more normal email on the other. We write down how much tidier, and which clue it used.

At the end we add up the "tidier" points for each clue across all the trees. The clue with the most points is the most important. In the spam example, the words "george" and "hp" win. George worked at a company called HP, and these were his emails, so if an email says "george," it's probably a real one.

Each split in a tree reduces the error by some amount, its gain. A feature's importance is the total gain of all the splits that used it, averaged over the trees, then scaled so the top feature is 100%.

Importance doesn't say which direction a feature pushes. "george" is important because its presence means "not spam." "ch!" is important because its presence means "spam." To see the direction you need the partial dependence plots in the next section.

The digit example (Fig. 18.8) shows a nice check: a forest separating 0s from 8s finds the middle pixels most important, which is exactly where an 8 crosses and a 0 has a hole.

  • Eq. 18.57 sums the impurity decrease \(G_j\) (weighted by node size) over the \(J-1\) internal nodes that split on \(k\). Eq. 18.58 averages over \(M\) trees. With one tree the scores are noisy and miss features that a different tree would have used. Compare 1 tree with 60 trees in the digit demo.
  • Caveats. Gain-based importance is computed on the training data, so it can reward features the trees used to overfit. It favors continuous or high-cardinality features, which offer more thresholds. Correlated features share credit: if two features carry the same signal, each may look half as important.
  • Permutation importance is an alternative: shuffle feature \(k\) in a held-out set and measure how much the loss gets worse. Switch the spam chart to it and compare.
  • MDI vs MDA. Eq. 18.57 is mean decrease in impurity. Permutation importance is mean decrease in accuracy. Strobl et al. (2007) showed the MDI bias toward variables with many split points. Conditional permutation schemes handle correlated features better.
  • Libraries. scikit-learn's feature_importances_ is MDI normalized to sum to 1, not max 100%. XGBoost offers importance_type of weight (split count), gain (average gain per split), cover, total_gain and total_cover, and these can rank features differently.
  • SHAP. TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al. 2020) computes exact Shapley values for tree ensembles in polynomial time. It gives per-prediction attributions that are consistent: a feature's attribution never goes down when the model comes to rely on it more, which gain importance doesn't guarantee.

Math, slowlyEquations 18.57–18.58

Adding up gains

  1. At internal node \(j\) with data \(\mathcal D_j\), the split on feature \(v_j\) has gain \(G_j=|\mathcal D_j|\,c(\mathcal D_j)-|\mathcal D_j^L|\,c(\mathcal D_j^L)-|\mathcal D_j^R|\,c(\mathcal D_j^R)\): the drop in total cost. For a regression tree, \(c\) is the mean squared error. For classification, Gini or entropy.
  2. Tree importance of feature \(k\): add \(G_j\) over the internal nodes that use \(k\). (Eq. 18.57) A feature never split on scores 0.
  3. Ensemble importance: average over the trees. (Eq. 18.58) For boosting, each tree's gains are reductions in the squared error of its pseudo-residuals.
  4. Report \(100\cdot R_k/\max_{k'}R_{k'}\).
The book notes that both the presence and the absence of a word can be informative. Importance is direction-free: it only counts how much splitting on the feature helped.

§18.6.2

Partial dependence plots: what does one feature do?

Once we know which features matter, we can ask how they change the output. The partial dependence of \(f\) on feature \(k\) averages out every other feature over the training data:

\[ \bar f_k(x_k)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^Nf(\mathbf x_{n,-k},x_k),\qquad \bar f_{jk}(x_j,x_k)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^Nf(\mathbf x_{n,-jk},x_j,x_k). \]

For a binary classifier, plot it on the log-odds scale \(\log p(y=1\mid x_k)/p(y=0\mid x_k)\). The two-feature version (Eq. 18.60) shows interactions: in the spam data, more "!" means more spam, but much more so when "hp" is absent.

Explain itAt your level

Pretend every email had the same number of "!" and see what the model says.

Take all the emails. Change each one so it has no "!" at all, ask the model how spammy each one is, and take the average. Then change them all to have a few "!", ask again, and average. Then a lot of "!". Draw a line through the averages.

If the line goes up, more "!" makes the model think "spam." If it goes down, like with "hp," more of that word makes the model think "real email."

To make a partial dependence plot for one feature, pick a value \(v\). Set that feature to \(v\) in every row of the data, leave the other features as they are, run the model, and average the outputs. Repeat for a range of \(v\) and plot.

The red ticks along the bottom (a "rug") mark the deciles of the feature in the data, so you can see where real data is. For spam features, most emails have a value of 0, so many deciles stack up there. The curve far to the right rests on few real emails.

The 2-D version does the same with a grid of values for two features and shows the average as a color map.

  • Eq. 18.59 is a Monte Carlo estimate of \(\mathbb E_{\mathbf x_{-k}}[f(x_k,\mathbf x_{-k})]\) under the marginal distribution of the other features, not the conditional one given \(x_k\).
  • Additive models. If \(f(\mathbf x)=\sum_kf_k(x_k)\), the PD for \(k\) is exactly \(f_k\) plus a constant. With an interaction, such as "!"×"hp" here, the 1-D PD shows the average of the "hp present" and "hp absent" curves, and only the 2-D plot shows the difference.
  • Extrapolation. Because the other features are not conditioned on \(x_k\), the average can include combinations that never occur, like a very high "hp" with zero "hpl." The model's output there is a guess.
  • ICE curves: plot \(f(\mathbf x_{n,-k},x_k)\) for each \(n\) separately. The PD is their average. Spread in their shapes reveals interactions.
  • Exact computation for trees. Friedman's recursion walks each tree once. At splits on \(k\) it follows the branch for \(x_k\), and at other splits it takes both branches weighted by their training fractions. This gives \(\bar f_k\) with no pass over the data (scikit-learn's method="recursion").
  • ALE plots (Apley & Zhu 2020) average local differences under the conditional distribution and avoid the extrapolation problem when features are correlated. Friedman's H-statistic uses PD functions to measure how much of the variance comes from interactions.
  • Causal reading. A PD plot shows the model's response, not the world's. Zhao & Hastie (2021) note it equals a causal effect (the back-door adjustment) only if the other features block all confounding paths and the model is correct.

Math, slowlyEquations 18.59–18.60

What the average does to an additive model and to an interaction

  1. Suppose \(f(\mathbf x)=a(x_k)+b(\mathbf x_{-k})\). Then \(\bar f_k(v)=\frac1N\sum_n[a(v)+b(\mathbf x_{n,-k})]=a(v)+\bar b\). The PD recovers \(a\) exactly, shifted by a constant.
  2. Add an interaction: \(f=a(x_j)+c\,x_j\,\mathbb I(x_k=0)+\dots\). Then \(\bar f_j(v)=a(v)+c\,v\,\hat P(x_k=0)+\text{const}\): the slope is a mix of the two groups, weighted by how common each one is.
  3. The 2-D PD keeps both: \(\bar f_{jk}(v,u)=a(v)+c\,v\,\mathbb I(u=0)+\text{const}\). The slope in \(v\) depends on \(u\), and that is the interaction. (Eq. 18.60)
  4. For a classifier, plot the model's log odds \(f\) (here the boosted score itself), not the probability. Averaging probabilities would squash the curves near 0 and 1.
